What’s going on

Why it works. Without losses, the wave equation is unchanged when t → −t. If every sensor re-emits its recording reversed in time, the wave retraces its path and converges on the source at the moment the original pulse left it, then spreads out again (no sink absorbs it).

Diffraction limit. The focal spot cannot be smaller than about λ/2: a perfect ring refocuses to J0(kr), 0.48 λ wide at half amplitude and 0.36 λ at half of p², the quantity measured here. A mirror that sees the wave from only one side yields a larger, elongated spot; scattering or reflecting walls send extra energy to the mirror and sharpen the focus (Fink’s time-reversal mirrors and chaotic cavities).

Here: interior attenuation is switched off (α = 0) so the simulation is reversible; the recordings are normalised and injected as sources at the sensor positions. The image is maxt p(x, t)² during the reversed run.

Equation. The 2-D constant-density acoustic wave equation ∂²p/∂t² = c(x, z)² ∇²p for the pressure p. Only P waves exist here; S and surface waves need the elastic solver of experiment 6.

Numerics. Second-order finite differences (leapfrog in time, 5-point Laplacian) on a 400 × 205 grid, computed live on your CPU (typed arrays). Courant number c·Δt/Δx ≤ 0.69 < 1/√2 and 13 grid points per dominant wavelength at v = 1. Sources are Ricker wavelets with a peak frequency of 7.6 Hz.

Units. The model is 10 km wide; v = 1 means 2.5 km/s, and a wave at v = 1 crosses the width in 4 s.
