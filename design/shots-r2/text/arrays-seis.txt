Fire the same quake for both sensors. Each arrival is a band across the record; its slope is the apparent slowness 1/vapp, so steep bands are slow waves. The fibre only feels strain along the cable: a wave that hits it broadside leaves almost no trace.

What’s going on

Seismometers record the pressure p at a point. A line of them is a record section: distance on one axis, time on the other.

DAS (distributed acoustic sensing) sends laser pulses down an optical fibre and measures the Rayleigh back-scatter from tiny flaws in the glass. Changes in the phase of the back-scattered light give the axial strain rate of the cable averaged over a gauge length G, every few metres along tens of kilometres of fibre.

Here: the cable is sampled every grid cell. In an acoustic medium (ρ = 1) the tangential particle velocity follows ∂vt/∂t = −∂p/∂s along the cable, and the strain rate is ε̇ = [vt(s + G/2) − vt(s − G/2)] / G. For a plane P wave arriving at angle θ to the cable, ε̇ ∝ cos²θ: maximal along the cable, zero broadside.

Equation. The 2-D constant-density acoustic wave equation ∂²p/∂t² = c(x, z)² ∇²p for the pressure p. Only P waves exist here; S and surface waves need the elastic solver of experiment 6.

Numerics. Second-order finite differences (leapfrog in time, 5-point Laplacian) on a 400 × 220 grid, computed live on your CPU (typed arrays). Courant number c·Δt/Δx ≤ 0.69 < 1/√2 and 13 grid points per dominant wavelength at v = 1. Sources are Ricker wavelets with a peak frequency of 7.6 Hz.

Units. The model is 10 km wide; v = 1 means 2.5 km/s, and a wave at v = 1 crosses the width in 4 s.
