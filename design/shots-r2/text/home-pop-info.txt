WHAT IS THIS WAVEFIELD?

×

Equation. The 2-D constant-density acoustic wave equation ∂²p/∂t² = c(x, z)² ∇²p. p is pressure, so only P waves exist here: no S or surface waves (those are in the Wave Lab's elastic experiment). Cyan is compression (p > 0), coral is rarefaction (p < 0).

Velocity model. Always drawn under the waves: red is slower than the background velocity (v < 1), blue is faster, and thin lines mark sharp interfaces. Recovering this structure from recorded waves is what seismic tomography does.

Source. Each click adds an acoustic point source to the pressure field: a Ricker wavelet (the second derivative of a Gaussian) with a peak frequency of 9.1 Hz, spread over a 5 × 5-cell Gaussian footprint. The magnitude only scales its amplitude.

Receivers. Each station samples p(t) at its position every time step (bilinear interpolation); the strip below shows the last 6 s. Place stations one by one, or as a line or ring array.

Numerics. Second-order finite differences (leapfrog in time, 5-point Laplacian) on a 480 × 300 grid, computed live on your GPU (WebGL2, 32-bit float textures). Courant number c·Δt/Δx ≤ 0.69 < 1/√2, about 13 grid points per dominant wavelength.

Boundaries. The outermost grid line holds p = 0 (Dirichlet). Absorbing edges add a 36-cell layer in which the equation gains a damping term σ(x)·∂p/∂t that ramps up quadratically, so outgoing waves fade instead of bouncing back (measured reflection: 0.3 % at normal, 0.7 % at 45° incidence). A free surface removes the layer on top: pressure vanishes there and waves reflect with reversed polarity, which is why "Along the top edge" puts receivers 6 cells below it. A reflecting box removes the layer everywhere. A weak uniform attenuation e−0.5 t lets the field calm down.

Not physical: magnitudes only scale the source amplitude, and coordinates are a cosmetic mapping of the screen onto Switzerland.
