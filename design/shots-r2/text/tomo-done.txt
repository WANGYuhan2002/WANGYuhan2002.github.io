The large structures are back. Bent rays follow the fast paths that first arrivals really take, so strong anomalies are less underestimated than with straight rays.

What’s going on

Forward problem. A travel time is the integral of slowness s = 1/v along the ray: t = ∫ s dl. With straight rays and the model split into cells, t = Σ Lj·sj, a linear system G·s = t with one row per source–receiver pair.

Inverse problem. There are more unknowns than independent data, so the solution is regularised: minimise ‖G·δs + τ − δt‖² + λd²‖δs‖² + λs²‖∇δs‖² by conjugate gradients (CGLS). τ is one constant time shift: it absorbs the bias of the automatic picker, which times the peak of the first pulse.

Bent rays. First arrivals take the fastest path, which bends around slow regions. In bent-ray mode the straight-ray model is the starting point; every ray is then bent towards the path of least travel time through the current model (pseudo-bending, Um & Thurber 1987), and the model is updated again: three linearised iterations.

Picking. For each trace the first-arrival window is predicted from the distance and the range of velocities. The pick is the first peak of the envelope (|p| averaged over one period) that reaches 15 % of the window maximum, refined to sub-sample precision by a parabola. Strong later arrivals focused by slow regions are skipped that way.

Equation. The 2-D constant-density acoustic wave equation ∂²p/∂t² = c(x, z)² ∇²p for the pressure p. Only P waves exist here; S and surface waves need the elastic solver of experiment 6.

Numerics. Second-order finite differences (leapfrog in time, 5-point Laplacian) on a 400 × 220 grid, computed live on your CPU (typed arrays). Courant number c·Δt/Δx ≤ 0.69 < 1/√2 and 13 grid points per dominant wavelength at v = 1. Sources are Ricker wavelets with a peak frequency of 7.6 Hz.

Units. The model is 10 km wide; v = 1 means 2.5 km/s, and a wave at v = 1 crosses the width in 4 s.
